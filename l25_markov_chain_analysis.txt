L2.5 Markov Chain Best-Response Dynamics — Explanation
======================================================

What this is
------------
The original L2.5 analysis builds epsilon-best-response (BR) graphs and measures
topological changes (edge churn, LSCC size, SCC entropy) when agents are
added/removed. That's purely structural — it tells you which edges exist, not
what happens when agents actually *play* best-response dynamics over time.

The Markov chain extension converts the BR graph into a stochastic process:
if the current "meta" is strategy i, agents switch to a best response to i.
When multiple best responses exist, the chain picks uniformly among them.
This gives the BR graph a dynamical interpretation.


How it works
------------
1. Transition matrix T: T[i,j] = 1/|BR(i)| if j is a best response to i.
   If i has no successors, it's absorbing (T[i,i] = 1).

2. Recurrent vs transient classification: Sink SCCs in the condensation DAG
   are recurrent classes (the meta cycles through them forever). Everything
   else is transient (visited at most finitely often).

3. Stationary distribution pi: Within each recurrent class, solve the left
   eigenvector equation pi * T = pi. When multiple recurrent classes exist,
   weight by absorption probabilities from a uniform start over transient
   states. Transient strategies get pi = 0.

4. Spectral gap: 1 - |lambda_2|. Larger gap = faster mixing = more volatile
   meta (the chain forgets its initial state quickly).

5. Mean first passage times (MFPT): Expected number of BR-dynamic steps to
   go from strategy i to strategy j. Only defined within recurrent classes.


Key findings (1000 bootstrap samples)
--------------------------------------

Stationary distribution vs MENE equilibrium:

  Strategy        pi (mean)    MENE sigma
  ----------------------------------------
  PSRO              0.378        0.325      <-- dominates the long-run meta
  PPO               0.171        0.377      <-- meta visits but doesn't linger
  OpenAI-5.2-Low    0.138        0.075
  OpenAI-5.2        0.131        0.046
  MAPPO             0.111        0.122
  NFSP              0.053        0.030
  EF1               0.018        0.019
  Walk              0.000        0.007
  Tough             0.000        0.000
  Soft              0.000        0.000

The main insight: pi and sigma disagree on PPO vs PSRO. MENE says PPO has
the highest equilibrium weight (0.377), but the Markov chain says PSRO is
where the meta actually *stays* (0.378). PPO is visited often but the chain
moves on quickly — PSRO is the attractor.

Walk, Tough, and Soft are transient in 100% of samples: the meta passes
through them but never returns. They're evolutionary dead ends under BR
dynamics.

Recurrence probabilities (P that a strategy is in a recurrent class):

  PSRO: 90%  |  PPO: 63%  |  OpenAI-5.2: 60%  |  MAPPO: 59%
  OpenAI-5.2-Low: 52%  |  NFSP: 34%  |  EF1: 7%
  Walk/Tough/Soft: ~0%

PSRO is almost always part of the recurrent core. The others fluctuate —
in some bootstrap samples they're in the cycle, in others they're transient.

Causal effects (delta-pi when adding each candidate):

  The strongest effects:
  - Adding PSRO: PPO's pi drops by 0.48, PSRO gains 0.38
    -> PSRO directly steals meta share from PPO
  - Adding Soft: PSRO's pi drops by 0.21
    -> Soft disrupts the PSRO-dominated meta even though Soft itself stays transient
  - Adding Tough: PSRO's pi drops by 0.31
    -> Similar disruptor role

  These are causal claims: do(add PSRO) changes the transition matrix,
  which changes pi. It's not just correlation in the payoff matrix.

Spectral gap: mean = 0.35, CI = [0.00, 0.83]
  High variance. Some bootstrap samples have near-zero gap (chain barely
  mixes — meta gets stuck), others have gap near 1 (meta cycles rapidly).

MFPT (recurrent core only):
  PPO -> PSRO:      3.2 steps (fastest — PPO quickly gives way to PSRO)
  PSRO -> MAPPO:    3.9 steps
  PSRO -> PPO:      5.6 steps
  MAPPO -> PPO:     6.8 steps (slowest — MAPPO doesn't easily lead to PPO)

Why this matters
----------------
The MENE equilibrium (sigma) answers "what's the Nash equilibrium of the
meta-game?" — a static concept. The stationary distribution (pi) answers
"where does best-response dynamics actually converge?" — a dynamic concept.
They can disagree, and the disagreement is informative:

- PPO has high sigma but lower pi: it's a strong equilibrium strategy but
  the dynamics don't stay there. Other strategies are best responses to PPO,
  so the meta moves on.

- PSRO has high pi and moderately high sigma: it's both an equilibrium
  strategy and a dynamical attractor. The meta keeps coming back to it.

The causal intervention (adding/removing agents and tracking delta-pi) goes
further: it tells you which agents are *responsible* for the meta structure.
Removing PSRO doesn't just remove its pi mass — it fundamentally changes
where everyone else ends up.
